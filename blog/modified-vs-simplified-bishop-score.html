Modified vs Simplified Bishop Score: What's the Difference?

The original, the modified (Calder) and the simplified (Laughon) Bishop scores side by side — components, ranges and the thresholds each one uses.

Quick answer

The modified Bishop score most often means the Calder version (1974): cervical length in centimeters replaces effacement and the maximum is 12. The simplified Bishop score (Laughon, 2011) keeps only dilation, effacement and station (0–9); a score above 5 predicted vaginal delivery as well as a full Bishop score above 8. Because each version has its own range and cut-off, always record which one you used.

The practical risk is mixing them up: a 6 is unfavorable on the original scale but already favorable on the simplified one. The full point values of the original score are in our Bishop score interpretation guide.

The modified Bishop score (Calder)

Calder, Embrey and Hillier (1974) replaced effacement — a percentage that has to be estimated — with the length of the cervix in centimeters. Position is scored 0 or 1 instead of 0–2, which gives a maximum of 12. The table below is the version reproduced by GPnotebook:

The published bands touch at their edges (for example 2–4 cm and 1–2 cm). Units that use this score usually define the boundaries in their own protocol — use that version.

The Queensland Clinical Guideline on induction of labour (2022) uses a modified Bishop score with cervical length and treats 7 or more as favorable, with ripening for 6 or less. Other modified versions exist: StatPearls describes research scoring systems with different weights for dilation and effacement, and notes that some studies used 5 or more as favorable on a modified scale. “Modified Bishop score” is therefore not one fixed instrument.

The simplified Bishop score (Laughon)

Laughon and colleagues (2011) proposed a shorter score without consistency and position. It keeps dilation, effacement and station, each scored 0–3 as in the original table, for a total of 0–9.

  • Cut-off: a simplified score above 5 is favorable.
  • Performance: it predicted vaginal delivery about as well as a full Bishop score above 8 (positive predictive value 87.7% vs 87.0% in their cohort).
  • Later use: Lee and colleagues (2022) found that a favorable simplified score (>5) after cervical ripening was associated with successful vaginal birth.

Which one should you use?

The one your unit's induction protocol is written around. ACOG's 2025 guideline on cervical ripening notes that there is no universally accepted definition of an unfavorable cervix, so a score is only meaningful next to the threshold it was designed for. When handing over or documenting, write the version, the total and the components, for example: “Bishop (original) 5: 2+1+1+1+0.”

The free calculator on this site and the Partus app use the original five-component score (0–13), interpreted with the common ≤6 / 7 / ≥8 bands.

Common questions

What is the modified Bishop score?

Usually the Calder (1974) version, which replaces effacement with cervical length in centimeters and scores position 0–1, for a maximum of 12. Other research versions with different weights also use the name.

What is the simplified Bishop score?

A three-item score from Laughon et al. (2011) using dilation, effacement and station (0–9). A score above 5 is favorable and predicted vaginal delivery about as well as a full Bishop score above 8.

What is a favorable modified Bishop score?

It depends on the version and protocol. Queensland's 2022 induction guideline treats 7 or more as favorable; some studies have used 5 or more. Check which modified scale your protocol refers to.

Is the simplified Bishop score as good as the full score?

In Laughon's cohort, a simplified score above 5 had a positive predictive value for vaginal delivery of 87.7%, compared with 87.0% for a full Bishop score above 8.
