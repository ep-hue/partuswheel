Naegele's Rule: Formula, Worked Examples and Practice Questions

How to calculate the due date from the last menstrual period by hand, why the answer can differ from a 280-day calculator by up to 3 days, and six practice questions with answers.

Quick answer

Naegele's rule: take the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP), add 7 days, subtract 3 months and add 1 year. Example: LMP January 10, 2026 → EDD October 17, 2026. It approximates the clinical convention of EDD = LMP + 280 days (ACOG CO 700). Because calendar months differ in length, Naegele's date is the same as LMP + 280 days or up to 3 days later. It assumes a regular 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14.

The formula

  1. Start from the first day of the last menstrual period.
  2. Add 7 days.
  3. Subtract 3 months.
  4. Add 1 year.

Adding 9 months and 7 days gives the same date. The rule is attributed to the German obstetrician Franz Naegele (1778–1851) and is widely taught as the bedside method in nursing and midwifery courses.

Why Naegele's rule and a 280-day calculator can disagree

Nine calendar months are not a fixed number of days. Depending on which months they span (and whether February has 28 or 29 days), nine months last 273 to 276 days. Adding 7 days therefore gives 280 to 283 days after the LMP. The largest gaps appear when the pregnancy runs through February.

Electronic calculators, including ours, count exactly 280 days, which is the convention in ACOG Committee Opinion 700. A 1–3 day difference from a hand calculation is expected and does not mean either is “wrong” — but the record should carry one agreed EDD.

Why add 7 days?

Because 9 calendar months fall about a week short of 280 days (40 weeks). The extra 7 days bring the total to roughly 40 weeks from the LMP — the 28-day-cycle model in which ovulation and conception happen around day 14 and the pregnancy lasts about 266 days from conception.

Calculating gestational age from the LMP

Gestational age is the number of days since the first day of the LMP, written as weeks + days. Example: LMP January 5, 2026; today March 2, 2026 → 56 days → 8 weeks 0 days.

Assumptions and limits

  • A 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14. ACOG CO 700 points out that LMP dating does not account for inaccurate recall, irregular cycle length or variable timing of ovulation. Johns Hopkins Medicine notes that dates may need adjusting for longer or shorter cycles.
  • Recall. ACOG CO 700 cites studies in which only about half of women accurately recall their LMP.
  • One length for every pregnancy. A 2021 review (Lawson) found that ethnicity, height, cycle variation, timing of ovulation, parity and maternal weight all influence pregnancy length, and concluded that Naegele's rule should be treated as a guideline.
  • Ultrasound comes first when it disagrees. First-trimester ultrasound is the most accurate dating method. When it differs from LMP dating by more than the ACOG threshold, the pregnancy is redated — see LMP vs ultrasound due date.

Practice questions

Original practice questions in the style of maternity nursing exams. Use Naegele's rule unless the question says otherwise.

1. A patient's LMP began on May 10, 2026. What is her EDD by Naegele's rule?

February 17, 2027. May 10 + 7 days = May 17; −3 months = February 17; +1 year = 2027. Note that LMP + 280 days gives February 14, 2027 — the February effect described above.

2. LMP December 28, 2025. What is the EDD?

October 4, 2026. December 28 + 7 days = January 4, 2026; −3 months = October 4, 2025; +1 year = October 4, 2026. The trap: adding 7 days moves into the next year before you subtract the months.

3. A patient's EDD is November 20, 2026. When did her LMP begin?

February 13, 2026. Reverse the rule: subtract 7 days (November 13), add 3 months (February 13), subtract 1 year (2026).

4. LMP January 5, 2026. What is the gestational age on March 2, 2026?

8 weeks 0 days. January 5 to March 2 is 56 days; 56 ÷ 7 = 8 weeks, 0 days.

5. LMP April 20, 2026. On November 9, 2026, which trimester is the patient in?

Third trimester. The gestational age is 29 weeks 0 days (203 days). The third trimester starts at 28 weeks 0 days.

6. Which assumption is built into Naegele's rule?

A regular 28-day menstrual cycle with ovulation on about day 14. Longer or shorter cycles, uncertain dates and early ultrasound findings can all change the final EDD.

Common questions

What is Naegele's rule?

A bedside method to estimate the due date: first day of the last menstrual period + 7 days − 3 months + 1 year. It approximates EDD = LMP + 280 days (40 weeks).

Why do we add 7 days in Naegele's rule?

Nine calendar months fall about a week short of 280 days. Adding 7 days brings the total to about 40 weeks from the LMP.

Why does Naegele's rule give a different date from an online due date calculator?

Calculators count exactly 280 days. Nine calendar months last 273–276 days, so Naegele's date is 280–283 days after the LMP — the same day or up to 3 days later, most often when the pregnancy spans February.

How accurate is Naegele's rule?

It assumes a 28-day cycle, ovulation on day 14 and accurate recall of the LMP. ACOG notes about half of women recall the LMP accurately, and first-trimester ultrasound is the most accurate dating method.

How do you calculate the LMP from the due date?

Reverse the rule: subtract 7 days, add 3 months and subtract 1 year — or subtract 280 days from the EDD.
